**This file is agreed at Hour 0 and it is the specification everything else is built to satisfy.** Seed data, tests and UI copy are generated to make these ten beats true. Read `00-PROJECT-CONTEXT.md` §9 alongside it — that table is the canonical beat list; this file is how you deliver it.

## 1. The frozen demo facts

Fill these in during the Hours 0–4 block and then **do not change them.** Every lane references this table. A name changed at Hour 30 costs three people an hour each.

| Slot | Value | Owner of the fact |
|---|---|---|
| Demo mandate, verbatim | *"₹50 lakh for maternal health and clean drinking water across Kalahandi, Nuapada and Balangir in Odisha, prefer partners with prior corporate CSR experience"* | All |
| Requirement units it decomposes into | 6 — {maternal health, WASH} × {Kalahandi, Nuapada, Balangir} | A |
| Hinglish mandate | *"humein rural Maharashtra mein girls ki education ke liye partner chahiye, budget 40 lakh"* | A |
| Keyword-invisible NGO | phrases its work as *"postpartum institutional delivery care"* — name: ________ | B |
| Top single match, trust score | ________ / ________ | B |
| Shell ring, three names | ________ , ________ , ________ | B |
| What the ring shares | registered address · trustee name · bank account | B |
| Consortium members (2) | ________ + ________ | A |
| Consortium budget split | ₹32,50,000 / ₹17,50,000 | A |
| Which member is capacity-capped | ________ (largest prior grant ₹8,00,000) | A + B |
| The coached NGO | ________ · scores **61** · reaches **80** after CSR-1 renewal + FY2024 audit | B |
| Named CSR deserts on the map | ________ , ________ , ________ | B |
| The ₹-at-risk number | ₹________ | C |

Two of those rows are load-bearing and are the ones teams get wrong:

**The consortium must be exactly two members.** Seed it so no single NGO covers more than three of the six requirement units, and so two specific NGOs are complementary across all six. Three-member combinations should exist and should *lose* on coordination cost — that is a better story than a three-way split, because you get to say "we evaluated the three-partner options; the coordination penalty made them worse." Two members also fit on one screen, and screen real estate at 1024px is a real constraint.

**The coached NGO must land on 61 and 80, not "about 60" and "about 80".** Vaishnavi's test asserts 61 ± 1 and 80 ± 1. Crossing from `Standard Audited` into `Verified Elite` at 80 is the badge flip, and if it lands at 79 the peak of your demo silently does not happen.

## 2. The four-minute script

Timings are targets, not aspirations. Rehearse with a stopwatch three times before you present. The first run will be six minutes; cut words, never beats.

**0:00–0:25 · The problem, one number**

> "Indian law requires large companies to spend two percent of profit on social causes. Every year a significant amount goes unspent or misallocated — not because companies don't want to spend it, but because finding a partner who is *legally eligible*, *financially clean* and *actually present in the district you care about* is a manual, weeks-long process. And a mistake is an audit finding."

Quote exactly one rupee figure here, pulled fresh from `csr.gov.in`, with the financial year on the slide. One correctly-cited number beats five vague ones, and a judge from this sector will check.

**0:25–0:40 · What SETU is**

> "SETU is a matching and verification layer for CSR. A company describes a mandate in one sentence; we return partners who are eligible, verified against their own documents, and geographically able to deliver — and where no single NGO can deliver, we assemble a consortium that can."

Then stop talking and start clicking. Everything after this point is demonstration.

**0:40–1:00 · Beats 1 and 2 — plain English in, structure out**

Click the preset. Do not type on stage. While the fields populate:

> "No forms. She said what she wants to fund. We pulled out the budget, two domains, three districts and a preference — and notice the confidence chips: we ask when we're unsure rather than guessing."

**1:00–1:30 · Beat 3 — the A/B toggle**

This is your first real point. Flip to Keyword.

> "Here's what a keyword search over the same corpus returns. Two organisations."

Flip to Semantic. Let the cards re-order.

> "Nine. And her best partner is this one, which keyword search missed entirely — because they don't write 'maternal health', they write 'postpartum institutional delivery care'. Same work. Different vocabulary. That gap is where CSR money gets misallocated."

**1:30–1:50 · Beat 4 — the deck and the latency number**

> "Each card carries a composite score, a trust badge, and cost per beneficiary. We benchmarked ranking at a thousand organisations — **[the p95 you measured at Hour 42]** milliseconds, end to end. What you're looking at here is our forty-eight fully-documented profiles."

Fill that number in from the actual benchmark and nothing else. Not a rounded-down guess, not last night's figure.

Say the benchmark number and the corpus size in the same breath. A judge who works out on their own that the deck has 48 rows while you claimed nine hundred has just decided to distrust every other number you say. Owning the distinction costs you two seconds and buys the rest of the pitch.

**1:50–2:20 · Beat 5 — evidence drill-down, the credibility beat**

Click the trust score. Click Compliance. Click the CSR-1 evidence item. The PDF page renders with the registration number highlighted.

> "Eighty-four. Four pillars. Click into compliance — and here is the actual filed CSR-1, page two, with the registration number we extracted, highlighted. Every point on that score traces to a document a compliance officer can open. This is the difference between a recommendation and a recommendation you can sign off on."

Pause here for two seconds. This is the beat that separates you from a scoring demo, and it needs air.

**2:20–2:45 · Beat 6 — the shell ring**

Open the High Risk NGO. The network panel appears.

> "Now this one. Its own documents are clean — formats valid, numbers plausible. But it shares a registered address, a trustee, and a bank account with two other organisations that present as unrelated. We didn't catch that by reading its paperwork. We caught it by looking at the network. All three are capped and flagged for human review."

Say *flagged for review*, not *fraudulent*. The restraint reads as domain seriousness, and it is the accurate claim.

**2:45–3:15 · Beat 7 — the consortium**

Go back and run the unfillable mandate.

> "Here the honest answer is that nobody can do this alone — no single NGO covers both domains across all three districts. So instead of returning a bad best-fit, we solve a coverage problem. Two partners, all six requirement units covered, eight percent overlap. Thirty-two and a half lakh to one, seventeen and a half to the other — and that second number is capped, because that organisation has never managed a grant larger than eight lakh. We also evaluated three-partner combinations; the coordination penalty made them worse."

The capacity cap is the most credible sentence in your pitch. It is the detail that tells a practitioner you spoke to someone who has actually disbursed a grant. Do not skip it to save time.

**3:15–3:40 · Beat 8 — the counterfactual coach**

Switch to the NGO role.

> "Everything so far served the company. This serves the other side. This organisation scores sixty-one, so it isn't getting recommended — and most systems would stop there, silently. We tell it exactly why, and exactly what to do."

Toggle CSR-1 renewal. The gauge animates to 80 and the badge flips.

> "Sixty-one to eighty. Verified Elite. And that number isn't an estimate — it's the same trust function the corporate side runs, called with one more document. We don't reject NGOs into a black box. We hand them the roadmap."

**3:40–3:55 · Beat 9 — the map**

> "Zoom out. Verified partner density against the Aspirational Districts. These three districts have high need and zero verified partners. That's where the money still isn't going — and it's a supply problem we can now name."

**3:55–4:10 · Beat 10 — the close**

> "Under a naive keyword process, ₹__ of this single mandate would have been routed to an organisation that is ineligible or high-risk. Same budget. Verified partners. Full coverage. Nothing sitting unspent."

Stop. Do not add a summary slide, do not thank the panel for their time twice, do not offer to show three more features. End on the number and let them ask.

## 3. The exact click path

Print this. Tape it to the laptop. The person driving follows it and does not improvise.

```
 1  Browser already open at localhost:3000, corporate role, zoom 125%
 2  Click preset "Odisha maternal health"          → fields populate
 3  Click "Find Partners"                          → deck renders
 4  Click ModeToggle → Keyword                     → 2 results
 5  Click ModeToggle → Semantic                    → 9 results + delta banner
 6  Click rank-1 card "View Dossier"               → drawer opens
 7  Click "Compliance 30%" pillar                  → evidence list expands
 8  Click the CSR-1 evidence item                  → PDF page renders, highlighted
 9  Close viewer, close drawer
10  Click the High Risk card                       → dossier → network panel
11  Close. Click preset "Unfillable mandate"       → Find Partners
12  Scroll to Consortium panel                     → coverage bars, donut, cap chip
13  Role switcher → NGO → select the coached NGO   → gauge at 61
14  Toggle "CSR-1 renewal"                         → gauge 61→80, badge flips
15  Nav → Map                                      → demand layer already on
16  Hover the three named desert districts
17  Nav → back to console                          → ₹-at-risk strip visible
```

Seventeen clicks. Rehearse until the driver does not look at the screen to find the next control.

**Two browser tabs, both pre-loaded** — one on the console, one on the map. Switching tabs is faster and safer than navigating, and it removes a route transition from your critical path.

## 4. Who says what

Whoever built a feature narrates it, so that a follow-up question is answered first-hand rather than relayed. That means the narration hands off mid-demo, which is fine and actually reads well — it demonstrates three people who each own something.

| Beats | Narrator | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Problem, solution statement, close | whoever is most coherent at Hour 44 | The framing needs energy, not knowledge |
| 3, 7 (toggle, consortium) | Piyush | He built the scoring and the optimiser |
| 5, 6 (evidence, shell network) | Vaishnavi | She built the parsing and the graph |
| 1, 2, 8, 9 (intake, coach, map) | third member | Built the interface and the interactions |

One person drives the laptop for the entire demo, and it is not always the person talking. Two people fighting over a trackpad in front of a panel is worse than any bug.

**Cross-train anyway.** At Hour 44 each of you explains a feature you did not build. Judges pick who to question and they do it deliberately — a panel that gets "I'd have to check with my teammate" twice concludes that one person built everything.

## 5. Failure recovery — decide these now, not on stage

| What breaks | What you do | What you say |
|---|---|---|
| Backend dead | Nothing. Fixtures take over automatically. | "We're running from cached responses — the whole console works offline, which we tested deliberately." |
| A page won't load | Reload once. If it fails again, skip the beat. | Nothing. Move to the next beat without comment. |
| Wrong results appear | Do not debug. Move on. | "Let me show you the piece that matters here." |
| PDF page won't render | Show the snippet callout instead. | "The extracted text with its page anchor — the page render is the same data." |
| Map is blank | Skip beat 9 entirely. | Nothing. Go straight to the close. |
| Laptop dies | Second laptop, already running, already at localhost:3000. | Nothing. |
| Projector won't sync | Present on the laptop screen, panel gathers round. | "Let's just do this on the laptop." |
| A feature is genuinely broken | Describe it as designed and specified. | "That's specified and partly built — I'd rather show you what's actually working." |

**The Hour 47 rule: whatever is broken at Hour 47 stays broken.** You demo around it. A confident walkthrough that skips one beat beats a panicked debug in front of a panel, every time. Nobody in the room knows what you planned to show.

Do not apologise for a skipped feature. Judges score what they see; they cannot score a thing you did not mention.

## 6. Q&A drill

Run this at Hour 45. One person plays a hostile judge working from `00-PROJECT-CONTEXT.md` §10 and does not go easy. Anyone who fumbles the weights answer or the "isn't this just cosine similarity" answer drills those two again immediately.

The five questions that will definitely come, in rough order of likelihood: *isn't this just cosine similarity*; *why those weights*; *is your data real*; *how do you verify the certificates are real*; *does it scale*. Full answers are in §10 of the context document — read them, then say them in your own words, because a recited answer sounds recited.

Three rules for answering. Answer in one sentence, then offer to show it — "happy to demo that" converts a challenge into another minute of demo time. Never say "we didn't have time"; say "that's the next thing we'd build" and mean it. And when you don't know, say so and say what you'd do to find out; a panel forgives a gap far more readily than a bluff, and they are very good at spotting the bluff.

**Have the weakest-point answer ready and give it honestly.** Ours is the synthetic dataset: the algorithms are tested, but the fraud patterns are ones we planted, so detection is proven in principle rather than in the field. Volunteering that answer well earns more credibility than any feature.
